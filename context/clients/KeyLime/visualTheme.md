# KeyLime Marketing — Visual Theme Guide

A structured set of visual theme directions for the KeyLime Marketing website, organized by where each is used and what archetype work it does. Every theme is paired with its reasoning so an AI agent — or a designer — understands *why* it fits, not just *what* to make.

The visual system itself (palette, type, tokens, components) is defined in `styleguide.md`; this document covers how to fill that system with imagery. The brand archetype is **the Everyman** and the design direction is **Modern Editorial**, both defined in `business-context.md`. Read all three together.

**The medium hierarchy.** Three image media carry this site, and they are not interchangeable: **real photography first, real product screenshots second, illustration third.** Section 8 is the decision guide for choosing between them — but the order matters from the first page mockup onward.

---

## 1. Photography themes

### 1a. Founder at work — the "real person" series

The single most valuable image set for this site. The Everyman archetype dies the moment the visitor suspects a faceless agency. A series of authentic founder photos in real working contexts:

- Founder at a desk with a laptop in a normal home office (not a curated workspace with succulents and a Mac)
- Founder on a client call, headset on, mid-conversation
- Founder shaking hands with a real client at the client's place of business
- Founder in a truck or car between client visits — phone in hand, sleeves rolled
- Founder with a notepad and a coffee at a local Cape Coral café
- Founder reviewing a printed report or sitting with a client looking at a laptop screen

**Why it works:** Reinforces "I build the sites, I run the campaigns, I answer the phone." Visitors should feel like they could call you tomorrow and you'd actually pick up.

**Avoid:** Studio portraits, glamour lighting, suit-and-tie corporate shots, anything that looks like a LinkedIn headshot from a Fortune 500 directory.

### 1b. Real local clients in their environments

Real photos of actual clients running their actual businesses. Each photo tells a small story.

- A garage-door tech installing a door
- A charter captain at the wheel with the SWFL coastline behind (Four Leaf Charters)
- A cabinet shop interior with finished cabinets (Verona Cabinets)
- A landscape crew working a residential job
- A roofing contractor on a ladder in the morning light
- Hands holding a phone showing a Google review notification

**Why it works:** Lets prospects see "people like me" already trusting KeyLime. Local context (palm trees, SWFL light, neighborhood streets) reinforces "from here, for here" without resorting to clichés.

**Avoid:** Stock photos of unrelated trades. Generic "construction worker" stock images. Anything that could appear on a Texas roofing company's website.

### 1c. Southwest Florida environmental texture

Photos that anchor the agency in its geography without being beach-vacation clichés.

- Cape Coral canals at golden hour (not sunset over the beach)
- A weathered local business storefront with hand-painted signage
- The Edison & Ford Winter Estates in Fort Myers
- Local farmer's markets with morning light
- Sanibel or Captiva working docks (boats, not tourists)
- Old Florida architecture — cracker houses, mid-century commercial buildings
- A weathered mailbox or bait shop sign with patina

**Why it works:** Establishes regional credibility through specificity. A visitor from Cape Coral will recognize these places; a visitor from anywhere else will feel the place even if they can't name it.

**Avoid:** Palm trees in front of pink sunsets, beach umbrellas, flamingos, Spring Break aesthetics, anything that looks like a tourism board ad.

### 1d. Behind-the-scenes work texture

Photos that show the craft of the work without being technical or alienating.

- A printed-out website wireframe with red pen markup
- A whiteboard with a campaign plan sketched in marker
- A laptop screen with a Google Analytics dashboard (out of focus, just enough to read the green-up-arrow)
- A notebook with handwritten client notes
- A second monitor showing real GBP listings
- Sticky notes on a wall mapping out a content calendar

**Why it works:** Signals craft and process without explaining the technology. The visitor sees "this person works through problems with a notebook, not a magic AI box."

**Avoid:** Code on a screen (alienates the Everyman audience), AI-generated abstract "data visualization" art, glowing keyboards, anything that looks like a tech-bro Instagram post.

## 2. Product & interface screenshots

KeyLime sells a *system*, not just services — the GHL-powered Toolbox: missed-call text-back, online booking, email & SMS marketing, database reactivation, the unified inbox, and automated review generation. For an offer like that, the most persuasive image is not a drawing of the concept — it is a clean, real, lightly-styled view of the actual product doing its job.

This matters because of the single biggest *silent* objection in a local-services sale: **"what am I actually buying?"** A skeptical home-service or beauty owner who has been pitched before doesn't want a metaphor for the product — they want to see the thing. A styled screenshot answers that question before they have to ask it, and it doubles as proof.

Screenshots are the **second pillar** of the visual system — after photography, ahead of illustration. Wherever a KeyLime service has a real screen behind it, a screenshot is the default, not an illustration.

### 2a. Styled device screenshots — the product in a phone

The highest-value screenshot on the entire site is a phone showing the missed-call text-back conversation. Build a small set:

- A phone showing the missed-call text-back thread — the automatic outbound reply, then the customer's response ("Need an AC quote")
- A phone showing an appointment confirmation and reminder SMS
- A phone showing a review-request text with the rating link
- A phone showing the unified inbox — texts, Facebook, Instagram, and Google messages in one stream

**Why it works:** A real customer conversation, in the customer's own words, is more convincing than any illustration of "communication." The buyer recognizes the interface instantly because it's their own phone. It removes uncertainty rather than decorating it.

**Execution notes:** These are not raw OS screenshots. Re-skin every capture into the Citrus & Charcoal palette — warm near-white screen, charcoal text, brand lime reserved for the outbound / KeyLime-owned message bubble only. Use real-sounding copy with a real local trade and a plausible business name (HVAC, salon, roofing). Never lorem ipsum. Never a flawless five-star fantasy — one believable exchange beats a perfect one.

### 2b. Annotated dashboard and desktop captures

The operator's-eye view — what the owner gets to see once KeyLime is running.

- The CRM lead pipeline / board
- The review feed, with Google stars accumulating over time
- A simplified automation or campaign builder
- A plain results view — calls recovered, no-shows down, reviews per month

**Why it works:** It shows the owner the control panel of their own business — concrete, legible, theirs. The **annotation** is what turns a screenshot into an explanation: two or three short callouts, no more, in the editorial label style (a thin lime rule or small lime dot plus an Inter label), pointing only at the things that matter.

**Execution notes:** Simplify aggressively. Crop to the single panel that tells the story; hide navigation chrome and secondary clutter. Recolor to the brand palette. Use real numbers, or numbers clearly marked as placeholder — never publish unverified stats (see `product-marketing-context.md`). A light browser or window frame is optional; if used, keep it minimal and flat.

### 2c. Before / after captures

The clearest possible articulation of value: the buyer's problem and the same screen after KeyLime, side by side.

- "Missed call, no follow-up" vs "missed call → auto-text → booked job"
- A near-empty Google review count vs a healthy, growing one
- A sparse week on the calendar vs a full one

**Why it works:** It pairs a screen the owner already recognizes as their problem with the identical screen solved. No claim is made out loud; the two images make the argument.

### 2d. The annotation system

So screenshots stay consistent wherever they appear, the callout treatment is fixed:

- A thin lime rule or a small lime dot as the pointer
- An Inter eyebrow-style label in charcoal, sentence case, kept short
- Two to three annotations per image maximum — more turns explanation back into clutter
- When the point of the annotation is a metric, set the number in italic Fraunces

This is the same editorial label language defined in theme 4b — screenshots borrow it so the annotated UI and the rest of the site read as one system.

**Why screenshots fit the Everyman archetype:** The Everyman doesn't oversell or mystify. Showing the literal product — plainly, with the seams visible — is the visual equivalent of plainspoken honesty. A faceless agency hides its product behind abstract illustration; an Everyman shows you exactly what you get and lets you judge it.

**Avoid:** Raw, un-restyled app screenshots whose default colors and OS chrome fight the palette. Cluttered full-dashboard captures with every panel visible. Fake or impossibly perfect data. Glowing 3D "SaaS hero" dashboards floating on gradients. Mismatched light/dark UI within one image set.

## 3. Illustration themes

When there is no real screen to show and no photograph that fits — abstract services, supporting graphics, empty states — illustration fills the gap. It is the **third** medium, not the first (see section 8). The Citrus & Charcoal palette and the Everyman archetype point toward a specific illustration sensibility.

### 3a. Hand-drawn linework — the "craftsman's notebook" style

Simple, slightly imperfect line drawings in charcoal (`--color-char-950`) on the warm near-white background, with brand lime (`--color-lime-500`) as the only fill or highlight color. Like a thoughtful person sketching in a notebook.

- A simple house outline with a wifi signal above it (Websites)
- A magnifying glass over a simple map pin (Local SEO)
- A megaphone with three soundwave arcs (Ads)
- A speech bubble with a star inside (reviews & reputation)
- A clipboard with a checkmark
- A coffee cup with steam rising
- An arrow that curves and lands on a target

**Why it works:** Imperfection signals human craft. The line weight should feel like a real pen, not a vector pen tool. Slight wobble is good. Perfect geometric icons feel cold and templated.

**Reference aesthetic:** Frank Chimero's illustration work, Christoph Niemann's New Yorker covers, the line-drawing style used in Basecamp's marketing materials.

### 3b. Editorial spot illustrations

Larger illustration moments for specific story beats — the kind of art an independent magazine might commission for a feature article. Two-color (charcoal + lime) with strong shape composition.

- A small illustration of a hand reaching out to a phone with a notification badge (lead generation)
- A house with a "for review" tag instead of "for sale" (reputation)
- A map of SWFL with three highlighted county outlines and small business icons scattered across them (local proof)
- A series of three small panels showing the process: meeting → building → growing
- A speech bubble containing five small star shapes

**Why it works:** Editorial illustration reinforces the magazine sensibility of the Modern Editorial direction. It also creates ownable visual moments — visitors will remember a specific illustration in a way they don't remember stock icons.

**Reference aesthetic:** The illustration style of The Atlantic's online articles, Reasons to be Cheerful magazine, Malika Favre's flat compositions but in the Citrus & Charcoal palette.

### 3c. Maps as design elements

The local credibility differentiator earns map-based imagery throughout the site.

- A simple line-drawn outline of SWFL with the three counties highlighted
- A map of Cape Coral with a small lime dot marking "we're here"
- A topographic-style map texture used as a subtle background pattern in one section
- An old-school nautical chart aesthetic for the Local Proof section

**Why it works:** Maps are inherently honest — they don't make claims, they just show where you are. Perfect Everyman device.

**Avoid:** Google Maps screenshots (legally and aesthetically wrong), 3D map renderings, anything that looks like a real estate ad.

## 4. Typographic & graphic themes

Visual moments built from type and shape rather than imagery.

### 4a. Large italic Fraunces pull quotes

The single most distinctive visual moment available with the chosen typography. Use italic Fraunces at 32–48px as a graphic element in its own right.

- Pull quotes from the founder placed in their own colored block
- Italic single-word headings used as section openers ("Finally." / "Honestly." / "Locally.")
- Large italic ampersands (&) used as decorative punctuation between section pairings
- Italic numbers as design accents in the process section

**Why it works:** Fraunces italic is genuinely beautiful and underused on the internet. Treating type as imagery creates ownable moments without commissioning art.

### 4b. Editorial label system

Inter eyebrow labels paired with thin lime underlines or short lime rules, like the labels in a thoughtful catalog.

- `WEBSITE DESIGN —————`
- `01 / WHAT WE DO`
- `LOCAL SEO · CAPE CORAL`
- Small lime pilcrow (¶) marks as section openers

**Why it works:** Reinforces the editorial magazine sensibility. Catalogs and magazines use these patterns; tech-SaaS sites don't. The contrast establishes the brand position visually. (This is also the annotation language used for product screenshots — see theme 2d.)

### 4c. Numbered marginalia

Small numbered annotations in the margins of sections, like footnotes or marginalia in a printed book.

- A small lime "1" next to a key sentence in the hero
- "See pricing →" as a lime marginal note next to a service description
- Small lime annotations marking key statistics

**Why it works:** Pure editorial signal. No tech-SaaS marketing site uses this pattern. The visitor's brain registers "this feels like something carefully made."

## 5. Color and texture themes

Backgrounds, textures, and color moments that build atmosphere without imagery.

### 5a. Warm paper textures (used sparingly)

A very subtle paper-grain texture overlay on one or two sections — not the whole page. Just enough to suggest "this was printed once, somewhere."

- Subtle grain on the testimonial section background
- A faint paper texture on the founder photo container
- A barely-there fiber texture in the footer

**Why it works:** Reinforces the editorial / craftsman sensibility. Warm paper is the visual opposite of glowing screens.

**Critical:** Texture must be very subtle. If you can consciously notice the texture, it's too strong. Test on multiple monitors — what reads as subtle on a calibrated display can look like noise on a cheap office monitor.

### 5b. Warm color grading

Photos can be lightly color-graded so a library stitched together from many sources reads as one set.

- All photos pulled gently toward the warm-neutral register of the Citrus & Charcoal palette
- Cool tones slightly muted so greens and blues harmonize with the near-white and lime rather than fighting them
- Skin tones kept natural and warm — never grayish or cool

**Why it works:** Visual cohesion across photography that comes from many sources. A single grading recipe applied to every photo makes a stitched-together image library feel intentional.

### 5c. Soft geometric shapes

Large, slow, soft organic shapes used as background elements (per the 2026 trend toward organic shapes, but applied with restraint).

- A single large soft circle of `--color-lime-50` placed behind a section title
- An organic blob shape in `--color-char-100` behind a stat card
- A long thin lime rule running diagonally through a section as a graphic anchor

**Why it works:** Adds visual warmth without imagery. Used sparingly, gives sections distinct personalities without committing to dark backgrounds or photography.

**Avoid:** Multiple shapes layered on a single section. Animated/moving shapes. Anything that looks like a Webflow template hero.

## 6. Iconography themes

### 6a. Single-style icon system — Phosphor or Lucide

Pick one icon library and use it everywhere. Phosphor's regular weight or Lucide at 1.5px stroke pairs naturally with Inter and the Modern Editorial sensibility.

- All icons in `--color-lime-700` on `--color-lime-50` containers
- 24px icons inside 48px containers
- Consistent stroke weight throughout
- No mixing of icon families ever

**Why it works:** Visual consistency. Mixing icon styles is the single most common amateur signal on agency sites.

**Avoid:** Material Design icons (too Android), Font Awesome (too dated), 3D icons, gradient icons, anything that looks like emoji.

### 6b. Hand-drawn icon variant

For moments where the standard icon system feels too clean, swap in hand-drawn alternatives in the same locations.

- The abstract-service icons could be hand-drawn instead of from a library — distinguishes the most important section
- Section opener icons could be hand-drawn for personality
- Process step icons could be hand-drawn

**Why it works:** Strategic imperfection in the most-viewed sections creates the "made by humans" feel. Less critical icons stay system-clean for usability.

## 7. Themes to deliberately avoid

Listing these as much for the AI agents reading this as for the founder. Anything below is an instant Everyman-archetype break.

- Stock business photography — diverse-team-around-laptop, handshake-over-conference-table, lightbulb-with-gears, hand-drawing-graph-going-up
- Raw, un-restyled product screenshots — default OS chrome and colors that clash with the palette and read as unfinished
- 3D-rendered objects floating on gradient backgrounds — the Apple/Stripe/Linear aesthetic
- AI-generated abstract art — the wavy gradient ribbons, the impossible architecture, the "AI art" look
- Glowing tech imagery — circuit boards, glowing keyboards, code-on-screen, neon, dark mode dashboards
- Cliché Florida imagery — palm trees, sunsets, flamingos, beach chairs, alligators, Spring Break
- Generic icon mash-ups — mixing 3 icon families on one page, gradient icons, colorful emoji-style icons
- Cinematic hero videos — drone footage of cities, slow-motion office scenes, anything that looks like a Squarespace template
- Glassmorphism, neumorphism, claymorphism — wrong era, wrong audience, wrong archetype
- Isometric illustrations of tiny people — the Slack/Dropbox/Asana marketing illustration style. Cold, generic, signals tech company

## 8. Choosing the right medium — photography, screenshots, or illustration

Three image media carry this site. They are not interchangeable; using the wrong one is a quiet credibility cost. The rule, in order:

1. **Photography first — for people, place, and proof.** Anything involving the founder, real clients, real work, or Southwest Florida is a photograph. Never an illustration of a person, never a stock substitute.
2. **Screenshots second — for anything with a real screen.** The GHL-powered services (missed-call text-back, booking, email & SMS, database reactivation, the inbox, review automation) all have a real interface. Show it. A styled screenshot beats a drawing of the same idea every time.
3. **Illustration third — for the abstract.** Services and concepts with no screen and no natural photograph — strategy, SEO, brand, process, empty states — are where illustration earns its place.

Applied to the services grid, this produces a consistent rule rather than one style stretched across everything: **the treatment is chosen by what each service *is*.** The GHL-powered service cards get styled screenshots (theme 2a/2b); the abstract service cards get line illustration (theme 3a). The grid stays visually coherent because every card carries the honest image for its own service.

## What to build first

You can't ship every theme above, and trying would dilute the brand. Build the smallest set that establishes the visual language, then grow outward.

**Tier 1 — Must have for launch:**

- Founder photo set (1a) — at least three real photos in real working contexts
- One real local environment shot (1c) — anchors the SWFL identity
- A starter screenshot set (2a) — at minimum the missed-call text-back phone, styled into the brand palette. This is the single highest-value screenshot on the site
- Single-source icon system (6a) — pick Phosphor or Lucide and commit
- Typographic moments (4a, 4b) — large italic Fraunces pull quotes and the editorial label system

That is enough to launch a homepage that is intentional, warm, locally credible, and concrete about what KeyLime actually delivers — without commissioning original illustration work.

**Tier 2 — Add as you grow:**

- Real client photos (1b) — collect as engagements mature and permission allows
- Annotated dashboard captures (2b) — for the homepage and service pages, once the product views are stable
- Hand-drawn icons for the abstract-service cards (6b) — once the design is stable

**Tier 3 — Long-term differentiators:**

- Custom editorial illustrations (3b) — commission once the brand is established and you want to deepen visual ownership
- Map graphics (3c) — particularly for the Local Proof section and an eventual service-area page

## Things worth flagging

**1. The single highest-impact visual decision is whether you appear in your own photos.** I cannot overstate this. The Everyman archetype is fundamentally about "you can trust this person because you can see them." Every founder-led local service business that gets this right has the founder visible. Every one that gets it wrong hides behind stock photography. If you do nothing else from this list, schedule a real photo session with a local photographer (not a portrait studio — someone who shoots editorial or documentary) and get 8–12 honest photos of you working. Budget: $400–800 in SWFL. Timeline: one afternoon. Single biggest visual investment available to you.

**2. Original photography — and real screenshots — are your strongest defense against the AI-image flood.** As of 2026, AI-generated imagery is everywhere and most visitors can subconsciously spot it. A real photo of a real person, and a real screen of a real product, are now the hardest visual assets to fake — which is exactly why they read as the most trustworthy. The Everyman archetype and the moment in time work together here.

**3. Screenshots must be real and re-skinned — never raw, never faked.** A styled screenshot is a proof asset; a raw OS capture that clashes with the palette looks unfinished, and invented data is the fastest way to lose a skeptical buyer. Re-skin every capture into Citrus & Charcoal, use believable copy and a real local trade, and mark any placeholder number as a placeholder until it is verified.

**4. Don't hand-illustrate everything yourself unless you're an illustrator.** The hand-drawn themes (3a, 6b) require actual drawing skill to land — bad hand-drawing reads as amateur, not warm. If you're not a confident illustrator, either commission a few key pieces from a real illustrator (Etsy, Behance, or local art schools are good sources for $100–500 commissions) or stick with the clean Phosphor/Lucide icon system. And remember the hierarchy: for any service with a real screen, a styled screenshot beats both an illustration and a hand-drawn icon — so the illustration budget only needs to cover the genuinely abstract services.
